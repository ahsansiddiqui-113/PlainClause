Most freelance money problems do not start with a client who refuses to pay. They start with a contract that never said where the work ends and when the money is due. Vague wording lets a small project grow quietly, and an unclear payment clause lets an invoice sit for months.

This guide walks through the clauses that cause the most trouble: unlimited revisions, fuzzy deliverables, missing change orders, late fees, payment on acceptance and kill fees. It is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ depending on where you live, so check local requirements and talk to a licensed professional if you are in a dispute.

## Unlimited revisions

A clause like this looks harmless when you are excited to land the job:

> Example clause: "Contractor shall make all revisions requested by Client until Client is completely satisfied with the Work."

Read it literally. The project is not finished until the client says so, and the client has no reason to say so. "Completely satisfied" is a feeling, not a measurable condition, and "all revisions" has no ceiling.

What to ask for instead:

- A fixed number of revision rounds, such as two, with a clear definition of a round (one consolidated set of feedback, not a trickle of emails).
- A rule that revisions fix problems against the agreed brief. New ideas or direction changes are new work.
- A turnaround window for feedback, so the project cannot stall for weeks and then restart.
- An hourly or per-round rate for anything beyond the included rounds.

## Vague deliverables

Scope creep usually hides in descriptions like "website redesign," "brand package" or "marketing support." Each phrase can mean ten things to the client and three things to you.

> Example clause: "Contractor will provide design services and related materials as reasonably required for the project."

The phrase "as reasonably required" gives the client the power to define the work after you have signed. A better scope names concrete outputs: number of pages, number of concepts, file formats, word counts, platforms, and who supplies content. It also lists what is excluded. A short exclusions list (for example, "hosting, photography, copywriting and ongoing maintenance are not included") prevents more arguments than almost any other sentence you can add.

## Change orders

A change order is a short written agreement that adjusts scope, price and timeline when the client asks for something new. Without a clause requiring one, you are left negotiating after you have already done the work, which is a weak position.

Look for language that says changes to scope must be agreed in writing and signed or confirmed by email before work begins. Also check for the opposite trap:

> Example clause: "Client may request modifications to the specifications at any time, and Contractor shall accommodate such modifications within the original fee."

That sentence turns every new request into free work. Replace it with a process: the client describes the change, you quote the extra cost and time, the client approves, and then you start. Even a two-line email approval counts for more than a verbal "sure, go ahead."

## Late fee clauses

Late fees are meant to nudge clients to pay on time. Check three things.

1. **The due date.** "Net 30" usually means 30 days from the invoice date, but some contracts count from the date the client receives or approves the invoice, which can push payment back.
2. **The fee itself.** Common structures are a flat fee or a monthly percentage of the overdue balance. Some places cap what you can charge, so check local rules before you set a rate.
3. **Whether it is mutual and clearly stated.** A late fee that is only mentioned on your invoice template, and not in the signed contract, may be hard to collect.

> Example clause: "Invoices unpaid after thirty (30) days shall accrue a late charge of 1.5% per month, or the maximum rate permitted by law, whichever is less, until paid in full."

The "whichever is less" wording is a good sign. It shows the clause was written with local limits in mind. Also consider adding a right to pause work on overdue accounts, because a late fee alone does little if you keep delivering while unpaid.

## Payment on acceptance

This is one of the sneakiest clauses in freelance work.

> Example clause: "Payment shall be due within thirty (30) days after Client's acceptance of the final deliverables."

Acceptance is controlled by the client. If they never formally accept, the payment clock never starts. Look for how acceptance is defined and whether there is a deemed-acceptance rule. A fair version says the work is accepted if the client does not send specific written objections within a set number of days, such as five or ten business days. Without it, you may be waiting on a decision the client has no incentive to make.

Also watch for "payment upon client's receipt of funds from its own customer," sometimes called pay-when-paid. That shifts the client's collection risk onto you. Many freelancers refuse this outright or limit it to a short, fixed backstop date.

## Kill fees

A kill fee is a payment you receive if the client cancels the project before it is finished. Without one, a cancellation can leave you with unpaid hours and an empty calendar.

> Example clause: "Either party may terminate this Agreement at any time for convenience. Upon termination, Contractor shall be paid only for Deliverables formally accepted prior to the termination date."

Because acceptance is rare mid-project, this clause can mean you are paid nothing for substantial work in progress. A fairer approach is payment for all work completed up to the termination date, plus a stated kill fee (often a share of the remaining fee, or a flat amount) that grows as the project advances. Also check what happens to the rights in unpaid work. Many freelancers keep ownership until final payment clears.

## A quick checklist before you sign

- Is the scope listed as concrete outputs, with exclusions?
- Is there a fixed number of revision rounds?
- Does every scope change require written approval and a price?
- Is the payment due date counted from the invoice date, with a deemed-acceptance rule?
- Is there a late fee, and is it within local limits?
- If the project is cancelled, do you get paid for work done, plus a kill fee?

None of these changes require an aggressive tone. Most clients accept reasonable terms when they are presented as a way to keep the project moving smoothly for both sides. If a client refuses every one of them, that is useful information in itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many revisions should a freelance contract include?

There is no universal number, but two rounds is a common starting point for creative work, with additional rounds billed at an hourly or per-round rate. What matters most is defining what counts as a round and stating that new direction is separate work.

### Can I charge a late fee if it is not in the contract?

It is risky. A late fee is much easier to enforce when both sides agreed to it in writing before the work began, and some places limit the amounts you can charge. Check local rules and consider adding the fee to your standard contract going forward.

### What is a reasonable kill fee?

It varies by industry and project size. Many freelancers combine payment for all work completed so far with a percentage of the remaining fee, and they increase that percentage as the project advances. Whatever you choose, write it down so both sides know the number before work starts.

### Do these clauses mean I need a lawyer for every project?

Not necessarily. Many freelancers use a well-written template and adjust it for each client. For large projects, unusual terms or any dispute, it is worth speaking with a licensed professional in your area.
