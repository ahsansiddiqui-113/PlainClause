Your rate is only half of what you earn. The other half is when the money arrives, how much of it arrives up front, and what happens when a client goes quiet. Two freelancers charging the same rate can have very different months depending on the payment terms they accepted.

Most freelancers negotiate price, then accept the client's template schedule. That is backwards. Payment terms are easier to move than price, because they cost the client far less, and they protect you from the situations that actually hurt: unpaid final invoices, projects that stall, and work done before any money has changed hands.

This playbook is general information, not legal advice. Rules on late fees, deposits and collections differ depending on where you live, so check local requirements and talk to a licensed professional if a payment dispute escalates.

## What you are trying to achieve

You are aiming for four things, in rough order of importance:

1. **Money before you start.** A deposit shows the client is committed and covers your early costs.
2. **Payment tied to progress.** Milestones keep your unpaid exposure small at every stage.
3. **A clear due date.** Counted from the invoice date, not from an approval you do not control.
4. **A consequence for lateness.** A late fee and a right to pause work.

You will not always get all four. The gated section below gives you a sequence for asking, the fallback positions to trade down to, and three email scripts you can copy.

## Who this is for

This works for solo freelancers and small studios taking project-based or retainer work from small businesses and mid-size companies. Large corporate clients often have fixed payment cycles set by procurement, so the plan includes a version for that case.

## Step 1: Know your numbers before you reply

Before you send any proposal, decide three figures.

- **Your walk-away exposure.** How many unpaid hours or dollars can you carry before the risk is uncomfortable? For many freelancers this is about one to two weeks of work. Write the number down.
- **Your ideal terms.** For example: 50 percent deposit, the balance split across milestones, net 14 on every invoice.
- **Your floor.** The weakest terms you would still accept for this particular client, for example 30 percent deposit and net 30.

Having a floor in advance stops you from conceding in the moment. If a client asks for something below it, you already know the answer: a polite no, or a higher price to compensate for the risk.

## Step 2: Put the terms in the first proposal

The easiest time to set payment terms is before the client has formed expectations. Put the schedule inside your proposal or quote, not in a separate conversation later. Present it as your standard process, not a request.

A clean structure for a mid-sized project is:

- Deposit due on signing, which starts the schedule.
- One or two milestone payments tied to delivered work.
- A final payment due on delivery, with files released on payment.

For short projects, a deposit and a final payment are enough. For long projects, switch to monthly invoicing so your exposure never exceeds a month.

State the due date in days from the invoice date, state the late fee, and add one line saying you may pause work on overdue invoices.

## Step 3: Anchor high, then trade

Ask for slightly more than your floor so that you have room to concede. A reasonable pattern:

1. Open with your ideal terms.
2. When the client pushes back, ask which part is the problem. Is it the deposit size, the timing, or the number of payments? People often object to one item and accept the rest.
3. Trade rather than give. If you reduce the deposit, shorten the milestone intervals. If you extend net terms, ask for the deposit to stay.

Never concede on two things at once. Each concession should buy something.

## Step 4: Fallback positions

Work down this ladder only as far as you need to.

**Deposit pushback**
- First fallback: a smaller deposit, but not below the cost of your first stage of work.
- Second fallback: no deposit, but a first milestone due within a few days of starting, and the project begins only after it is paid.
- Last resort: no deposit and a short first milestone, with a clear pause right.

**Net terms pushback**
- First fallback: move from net 14 to net 30, in exchange for keeping the deposit.
- Second fallback: net 30 on milestones, but payment on delivery for the final invoice.
- Do not accept: payment on acceptance without a deemed-acceptance date, or payment only after the client is paid by its own customer.

**Late fee pushback**
- First fallback: reduce the percentage, but keep a fee.
- Second fallback: replace the fee with a right to pause work and withhold the final files until paid.
- Many clients will accept a pause right more readily than a fee, and it is often the stronger protection.

**Large-company clients**
- Ask for their payment calendar up front, and invoice immediately after each milestone.
- Ask whether a purchase order number is needed, since invoices without one are often delayed.
- If net 60 is non-negotiable, raise your rate or request a larger deposit to cover the financing cost.

## Step 5: Get it in writing the same day

After any call, send a recap email listing what was agreed. If the client does not correct it, you have a written record. Then make sure the signed contract matches the email, including the due date, deposit amount and the pause right.

## Step 6: Enforce quietly and early

Terms only matter if you apply them. A simple routine:

- Send the invoice the day the milestone is delivered.
- Send a friendly reminder a few days before the due date.
- On the day after the due date, send a polite note with the invoice attached.
- A week after that, remind the client of the pause right and the late fee.
- Pause work if the invoice remains unpaid, and say so in writing.

Keep every message calm and factual. If the amount is significant, check local options for recovering it and consider a licensed professional.

## Email scripts you can copy

Adjust the numbers and names to fit your situation.

### Script 1: Presenting your terms with the proposal

> Subject: Proposal and payment schedule for the website project
>
> Hi Jordan,
>
> Thanks for the conversation yesterday. I have attached the proposal. For payment, my standard process for projects of this size is a 50 percent deposit on signing, 25 percent when the first designs are delivered, and the final 25 percent on delivery, before the files are released. Invoices are due within 14 days of the invoice date. Overdue invoices carry a late fee of 1.5 percent per month, and I may pause work until they are settled.
>
> If anything in the schedule does not work for your team, let me know which part and I am happy to talk it through. I would love to get started as soon as the deposit lands.
>
> Best,
> Sam

### Script 2: Responding to "we pay everything net 60 after delivery"

> Subject: Re: Payment terms
>
> Hi Jordan,
>
> Thanks for explaining how your accounts team works. Net 60 on the full amount is longer than I can carry for a project this size. Here is what I can offer: I can accept net 30 on milestone invoices, provided we keep a 30 percent deposit on signing so I can reserve the time on my calendar. If net 60 is truly fixed on your side, I would need to adjust the project fee to account for the longer wait.
>
> Which of those two options is easier for you to take to your finance team? Happy to jump on a quick call if that helps.
>
> Best,
> Sam

### Script 3: First reminder after a missed due date

> Subject: Invoice 1042, now past due
>
> Hi Jordan,
>
> A quick note that invoice 1042 for the second milestone was due on the 14th and I have not yet seen payment. I have attached it again for convenience. Could you let me know when I can expect it, or whether anything is holding it up on your side?
>
> As a reminder, our agreement lets me pause work on overdue invoices, so I will hold the next stage until this is settled. I would prefer to keep things moving, so please tell me if I can help with anything, such as a purchase order number or a different billing contact.
>
> Thanks,
> Sam

## When to walk away

If a client refuses a deposit, refuses written terms and also wants a rush deadline, the problem is not the paperwork. It is the risk profile. Raising your price, shrinking the first phase, or declining the work are all legitimate responses. Protecting your cash flow is part of running a sustainable freelance business.

## Final checklist

- Do you know your exposure limit and your floor?
- Are payment terms inside the first proposal?
- Does each concession buy something in return?
- Is the due date counted from the invoice date?
- Does the contract include a pause right, not just a late fee?
- Did you send a written recap after every call?
- Do you have a reminder routine you actually follow?
