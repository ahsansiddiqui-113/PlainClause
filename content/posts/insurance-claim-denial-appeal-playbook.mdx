A denial letter feels final, but it is often just the insurer's first position. Claims get denied or underpaid for many reasons: a missing document, a disagreement about the cause of the damage, an estimate that is too low, a policy exclusion that may or may not fit your situation. Some of those reasons hold up. Many can be challenged with better evidence and a clearer argument.

This playbook walks through a structured way to respond. It is general information, not legal or insurance advice, and policies, deadlines and complaint channels differ depending on where you live and what coverage you have. Always check your own policy and your local rules.

## Start by understanding what actually happened

Before you write anything, work out which of these you are facing:

- **A full denial.** The insurer says the loss is not covered at all.
- **A partial denial or underpayment.** They accept the claim but pay less than you expected, often because of the repair estimate, depreciation, or a deductible.
- **A delay.** No decision, or repeated requests for the same documents.
- **A cancellation or non-renewal tied to the claim.** This is a different issue with its own rules.

Each calls for a slightly different response. In every case the denial letter is your starting document. It should state the reason, name the policy provisions relied on, and explain how to appeal. Read it three times and highlight the exact words the insurer uses.

## The step-by-step plan

Treat this as a project with dates. Write the dates on a single page and keep it at the front of your claim folder.

### Day 0 to 3: Lock down the deadlines

1. Find the date on the denial letter and the appeal deadline, if it states one. If it does not, ask in writing.
2. Look in your policy conditions for any deadline to dispute a claim or take legal action. Some are surprisingly short, sometimes a year from the date of loss. Many places have rules that can change these, but do not assume that without checking.
3. Put every deadline in your calendar with reminders two weeks and one week before.

### Day 1 to 7: Request the claim file and the policy

You are generally entitled to a complete copy of your policy, and in many places you can ask for the documents the insurer relied on, such as adjuster notes, engineer reports and photos. Ask in writing and keep proof of sending. Script 1 below does this.

### Day 3 to 21: Build the evidence file

Create a folder, physical or digital, with these sections:

- **The loss itself.** Dated photos and video, ideally including the first images you took. Weather reports or incident reports if relevant.
- **Condition before the loss.** Older photos, inspection reports, maintenance receipts, listing photos, or purchase records. These counter arguments about wear and tear or prior damage.
- **Cost evidence.** Written repair estimates from at least two licensed contractors, with line items, not just totals. Receipts for emergency repairs and extra living costs.
- **Value evidence.** For belongings: receipts, bank statements, serial numbers, product pages, and any inventory you made.
- **Communications log.** A running list of every call: date, time, person, what was said. Follow calls with a short email summarizing them.

### Day 7 to 21: Read the policy like an adjuster

Go through the policy in this order:

1. **Declarations page.** Confirm the coverage, limits and deductibles match what the insurer applied.
2. **Insuring agreement.** Does it promise to pay for this type of loss?
3. **Exclusions.** Find the one the insurer cited. Read the whole paragraph, including any exceptions, which often start with words like "unless," "except" or "however."
4. **Definitions.** Check how the policy defines the key terms in the denial.
5. **Conditions.** Did the insurer say you missed a requirement? Compare that against what you actually did, with dates.
6. **Endorsements.** These can change everything above, so read them last and carefully.

Write one short paragraph for each denial reason: what the insurer says, what the policy actually says, and what your evidence shows. If you cannot find a gap, consider whether the denial may be correct, or whether a different coverage on your policy fits.

### Day 14 to 30: Send the formal appeal

Send the appeal well before the deadline, by a trackable method, and also by email if the insurer accepts it. Keep it focused: one to two pages, calm, specific, with an exhibit list. Script 2 is a model.

### Day 30 to 60: Follow up and escalate

Insurers often have a set period to respond to an appeal, and your region may have rules on this. If you hear nothing, follow up in writing. If the answer is still no, or the response is a stall, escalate. Script 3 shows how. Typical escalation routes include the insurer's own appeals or complaints department, your state or provincial insurance regulator or equivalent body where you live, and in some places a formal dispute resolution program. Look up the right channel for your location, because the names and rules differ.

### Beyond 60 days: Consider outside help

- **Independent adjusters or public adjusters** can prepare an estimate and negotiate for a fee, often a percentage of the recovery. Check licensing rules where you live and read their contract carefully.
- **Engineers or other experts** can provide a written cause-of-loss opinion, which can be powerful when the denial rests on a technical claim.
- **A lawyer who handles insurance disputes** can advise on deadlines, rights and options. Many offer an initial consultation, and some work on contingency, but terms vary.

Some policies include an appraisal clause for disputes about the amount of a loss. If yours does, it may offer a faster path than a lawsuit, but it comes with its own rules and costs, so read it before invoking it.

## Script 1: Request for the claim file

Send this within the first week.

> Subject: Request for claim file and policy copy - Claim [number]
>
> Dear [Claims Representative],
>
> I am writing about claim number [number] under policy number [number], for a loss that occurred on [date] at [address or vehicle].
>
> I received your letter dated [date] [denying / partially paying] this claim. To review the decision, please send me the following within [14] days: a complete certified copy of my policy including all endorsements; all adjuster notes, reports, photographs, estimates and correspondence relating to this claim; and any engineer, contractor or other third-party reports used in your decision.
>
> Please also confirm in writing the deadline and the process for appealing this decision.
>
> Thank you,
> [Name, address, phone, email]

## Script 2: Formal appeal letter

> [Date]
>
> [Insurer name, Appeals Department, address]
> Sent by [trackable method] and email
>
> Re: Appeal of claim decision. Claim [number], Policy [number], Date of loss [date]
>
> Dear Sir or Madam,
>
> I am appealing your decision dated [date] to [deny / pay only part of] my claim, and I ask that it be reviewed by someone who was not involved in the original decision.
>
> Your letter states that the loss is not covered because [quote the insurer's stated reason]. I respectfully disagree for the following reasons.
>
> First, the policy provision you cite states: [quote the exact wording]. The evidence shows that [explain, for example, the damage was sudden and caused by the storm on that date, as shown in Exhibits A and B].
>
> Second, [if relevant: the policy contains an exception that applies, quoting it, or the insurer's estimate omits items that are included in the attached licensed contractor estimates, Exhibits C and D, totaling $[amount]].
>
> I have enclosed the following exhibits: [list each with a one-line description].
>
> I request that you reverse your decision and [pay the claim in full / pay the additional amount of $X]. Please respond in writing within [30] days, or sooner if your policy or local rules set a shorter period. If this is not resolved, I intend to pursue the other options available to me, including a complaint to the appropriate insurance regulator.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Name, contact details]

## Script 3: Escalation email

Use this if the appeal is denied or ignored. Send it to the insurer's complaints or customer relations contact, and keep a copy for any regulator complaint.

> Subject: Escalation - unresolved claim [number], appeal dated [date]
>
> Dear [Name or Complaints Department],
>
> I submitted a formal appeal on [date] regarding claim [number]. [I have not received a response / I received a response dated [date] that did not address the points I raised.]
>
> The key issue remains: [one or two sentences, for example, the policy covers sudden water damage, and the plumber's report confirms a sudden pipe failure].
>
> I am asking for a written decision from a supervisor or complaints manager by [date, about 14 days out]. Attached are my original appeal and exhibits, and a timeline of contacts.
>
> If I do not receive a satisfactory response by that date, I plan to file a complaint with the insurance regulator in my area and consider other options. I would prefer to resolve this directly.
>
> Regards,
> [Name, phone, email, claim and policy numbers]

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Waiting until the last week of a deadline to start.
- Making the appeal about frustration instead of evidence and policy wording.
- Missing the paper trail: phone calls without a follow-up email.
- Signing a release or accepting a final payment without understanding whether it closes the claim. Ask in writing what any payment is for before you cash or sign.
- Throwing away damaged items or making permanent repairs before documenting, unless needed to prevent further damage. If you must act to protect the property, keep receipts and photos.
- Assuming you have no case because the first answer was no.

## Final checklist

1. Deadlines in the calendar.
2. Claim file and policy requested.
3. Evidence folder built, with two or more estimates.
4. Denial reasons matched to exact policy wording.
5. Appeal sent by trackable method with an exhibit list.
6. Follow-up and escalation dates set.
7. Outside help considered if the amount at stake justifies it.

Persistence, organization and calm, specific writing tend to matter more than the strength of your feelings. Keep it factual and keep everything in writing.
