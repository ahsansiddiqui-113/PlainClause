Signing up for a gym or a subscription usually takes two minutes. Leaving can take weeks of planning. The difference comes from a handful of clauses that quietly set up automatic renewal, restrict how and when you can cancel, and let the company change the price. This guide explains what each one means and what to look for before you sign.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules on automatic renewals vary by place, and many places have consumer protection laws that add requirements for the company, so check your local rules and your own contract.

## The basic renewal clause

Most auto-renewing agreements share a core idea: the contract continues unless you stop it. Here is a fictional example of a membership clause:

> **Example only:** "This Membership Agreement has an initial term of twelve (12) months. At the end of the initial term it will automatically renew for successive twelve (12) month terms at the then-current rate unless Member cancels as provided in Section 9."

Notice the words "then-current rate." That means the price for the next term is whatever the company is charging at that time, not what you paid originally. And "as provided in Section 9" tells you that the real rules are somewhere else. Always follow those cross-references.

## Cancellation windows

The cancellation clause is where most people get caught. Look at the details:

> **Example only:** "To cancel, Member must deliver written notice to the Club not less than thirty (30) days and not more than sixty (60) days before the end of the then-current term. Cancellation requests outside this window are void."

That is a narrow window: a thirty-day period, opening sixty days out and closing thirty days out. Ask yourself:

- **When does the clock start?** Some clauses count from the date of the notice, others from when the company receives it.
- **Is there an opening date as well as a closing date?** A request that is too early may be rejected.
- **What happens at the next billing date?** Some companies bill monthly, so you may still owe a full month after you cancel.

A practical tip: put the window dates in your calendar the day you sign, with a reminder a week ahead of the opening date.

## "Cancel by certified mail" and in-person-only clauses

Many companies make it easy to join online but require something old-fashioned to leave:

> **Example only:** "Cancellation will be effective only if made in writing and sent by certified mail, return receipt requested, to the corporate address listed on the signature page. Verbal, email or online cancellation requests will not be accepted."

If your contract says this, treat it seriously. Some places have rules that require companies to offer a cancellation method comparable to the way you signed up, or that limit certain restrictive methods, but those rules vary and are not guaranteed to apply to your situation. The safest approach is to do both: follow the method in the contract exactly, and also send a short email or use the app, so you have a record that you tried.

If you are sending a cancellation letter:

1. Keep it brief: your name, member or account number, the date, and a clear statement that you are cancelling.
2. Send it by the method the contract names, and keep the receipt and tracking number.
3. Photograph or scan the letter before mailing.
4. Ask in writing for confirmation that the membership is cancelled and the last billing date.
5. Check your card or bank statements afterward to confirm charges stopped.

## Price-change clauses

Even a low introductory price can change. A typical clause says:

> **Example only:** "Company may modify membership dues, fees and assessments at any time upon posting notice at the facility or on its website. Continued use of the services constitutes acceptance of the modified rates."

Two phrases to notice. "At any time" means there may be no cap on how often the price can change. "Continued use constitutes acceptance" means that if you keep going, you are treated as agreeing. Better terms include a fixed price for the initial term, a cap on increases, or a right to cancel without penalty if the price goes up. You can ask whether the company will agree to any of these, and get the answer in writing.

Also watch for add-on charges that are separate from the monthly price, such as an annual maintenance fee, a one-time "enhancement" fee or a processing charge. Those are often introduced under the same "modify fees" language.

## Free trials and promotional rates

Free trial offers usually convert into paid plans if you do not act. Read the confirmation email for the trial length, the date billing starts and the price afterwards. If you cannot find those details, that is a sign to be careful. Set a reminder for a day or two before the trial ends, and cancel early if you are not sure you want it. In many cases cancelling early does not forfeit the remaining trial time, but check the terms.

## Moving, illness and other exceptions

Some contracts allow early termination for specific reasons, often a move beyond a certain distance, a documented medical condition, or military deployment. These exceptions are frequently buried and often require paperwork, sometimes a letter from a doctor or proof of a new address, delivered within a set time. If any of these might apply to you, ask for the exceptions in writing before you sign, and ask what documents they require.

## What to ask before you sign

1. Does this renew automatically, and for how long each time?
2. How exactly do I cancel, and is there a cancellation window?
3. Is there a cancellation fee, and when is the last payment due?
4. Can the price change during my term, and can I cancel if it does?
5. What fees apply beyond the monthly rate?
6. Can I pause or freeze the membership instead, and for how long?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a gym force me to cancel by certified mail?

Many contracts say so, and if you signed it, the company may insist on that method. Some places have consumer rules that limit overly burdensome cancellation methods, but these differ widely. The safest approach is to follow the method in the contract exactly, keep proof of delivery, and also send a written request by email so there is a record.

### What happens if I miss the cancellation window?

You may be bound to another full term at the current rate. It is still worth contacting the company in writing right away, explaining the situation and asking for a one-time exception or a shorter term. Some companies will agree, especially if you ask promptly and politely. Also check whether your place has rules that give consumers extra cancellation rights.

### Can a subscription raise its price whenever it wants?

Only if the contract allows it, and many do. Look for language about modifying fees at any time or after notice. Better contracts require advance notice and give you the right to cancel if the price rises. If notice is required and you did not get it, raise that with the company in writing.

### Does cancelling online count if the contract says mail only?

Not necessarily. The contract language usually controls unless a local rule says otherwise. If you cancel online, also follow the written method in the agreement, and keep screenshots, confirmation emails and dates. Check your next statement to make sure the charges stopped.
