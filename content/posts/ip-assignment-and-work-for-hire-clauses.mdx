When you create something for someone else, such as code, designs, writing or an invention, a few sentences in your contract decide who owns it afterward. Those sentences are easy to skim past, and they can matter more than your rate.

This guide covers the three most common ownership clauses: work-for-hire, IP assignment and invention assignment. It is general information, not legal advice. How these clauses are treated differs depending on where you live and the type of work, so check local rules and talk to a licensed professional about anything high stakes or disputed.

## Three clauses that sound alike but work differently

### Work-for-hire

A work-for-hire clause says the client, not you, is treated as the creator of the work from the start. It is a concept that exists in some legal systems and is limited in how it applies, and in many places it works differently for employees than for independent contractors. That is why contracts often pair it with a backup assignment clause, in case the work-for-hire label does not apply.

> Example clause: "All Work Product shall be deemed work made for hire. To the extent any Work Product is not work made for hire, Contractor hereby irrevocably assigns to Client all right, title and interest in and to the Work Product."

### IP assignment

An assignment transfers ownership you would otherwise have. The words that matter are "hereby assigns" (ownership transfers now) versus "agrees to assign" (a promise to transfer later). Also note the timing: does ownership move when the work is created, or only after you are paid?

### Invention assignment

Common in employment agreements, this clause covers inventions, ideas and improvements you develop. A fair version limits itself to things related to the company's business or made with company time and resources. An overbroad one claims everything you create during employment.

> Example clause: "Employee assigns to the Company all inventions, works and ideas conceived or developed by Employee during the term of employment, whether or not during working hours and whether or not related to the Company's business."

That sentence reaches your weekend hobby project. If you build anything on the side, this is the clause to read twice.

## Five things to check

1. **When ownership transfers.** For freelancers, the best practice is transfer upon full payment. If the clause transfers ownership immediately, a client who never pays still owns your work.
2. **What counts as Work Product.** Does it cover only the deliverables named in the project, or also drafts, notes, ideas and anything related? Narrower is better for you.
3. **Your pre-existing materials.** You probably use templates, libraries, fonts, frameworks or methods you made before this client. If the clause is silent, the client may argue they own those too.
4. **Third-party materials.** Stock images, open source components and licensed fonts cannot usually be assigned. A clause that promises the client full ownership of everything can put you in breach.
5. **Your right to show the work.** Many assignments take away your ability to put the project in your portfolio unless you reserve that right.

## Protecting your pre-existing work

> Example clause: "Contractor grants Client a non-exclusive, perpetual license to use any Contractor tools, templates or components incorporated into the Work Product, solely as part of the Work Product."

This is the pattern to look for. You keep ownership of your underlying tools and give the client a license to use them as part of the delivered work. It is fair to both sides: the client can use what they paid for, and you can reuse your own building blocks on the next job.

To make this explicit, add a short schedule listing the tools and materials you bring in. A list dated before the project starts is helpful evidence of what was yours.

## For employees: carve-outs and prior inventions

Many employment contracts include a place to list prior inventions you own and want excluded. Fill it in honestly and specifically. If you leave it blank, you may be agreeing that you have none.

Also check whether your side projects are protected. Some places limit how far an employer can claim inventions made on your own time without company resources, and some contracts add a similar carve-out voluntarily. Look for wording like "developed entirely on my own time, without using company equipment, supplies, facilities or confidential information, and not relating to the company's business." If it is missing, you can ask for it. If you plan to launch something on the side, get the employer's written confirmation that it is outside the clause.

## Moral rights, credit and licensing back

Even after ownership moves, you may still care about credit and reuse. Consider asking for:

- A right to display the work in your portfolio and describe your role.
- A license back to reuse general techniques, and non-confidential components, in future projects.
- Credit or attribution if the work is public-facing and attribution matters to you.

A client that is not worried about owning your tools will usually agree to these.

## How to ask for changes

You rarely need to reject the whole clause. Targeted edits are easier to get approved:

1. Change "upon creation" to "upon full payment."
2. Replace "all work and ideas" with "the deliverables described in the statement of work."
3. Add a sentence reserving your pre-existing materials and granting a license for their use inside the deliverables.
4. For employees, add a prior inventions list and a side project carve-out.
5. Reserve the right to show the work in your portfolio.

A simple note works: "I am comfortable assigning ownership of the final deliverables. I would like to clarify that my pre-existing tools remain mine and that transfer happens on full payment."

## Frequently asked questions

### Does work-for-hire mean the client automatically owns everything I make?

Not always. Whether work-for-hire applies depends on where you live, the type of work and whether you are an employee or a contractor, which is why contracts usually add a backup assignment. Read the exact wording and get local advice for important projects.

### Should I assign ownership before I am paid?

Most freelancers avoid it. Tying the transfer of ownership to full payment gives you leverage if the client stops paying, and many clients accept that wording because they only need ownership once they have paid.

### Can my employer own my side projects?

Only if the contract and local rules allow it. Broad invention assignment clauses can sweep in work done on your own time, so look for carve-outs and list prior inventions. Check local rules and consider talking to a licensed professional before relying on a side project exclusion.

### What if I used open source code or stock assets in the project?

You generally cannot assign ownership of materials you do not own. Disclose them in the contract and state that third-party materials stay under their own licenses.
