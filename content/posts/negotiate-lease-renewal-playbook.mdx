Most tenants accept the first renewal offer they receive. It arrives as a short letter or an email with a new rent figure and a signature line, and it feels like a take-it-or-leave-it notice. It is not. A renewal offer is an opening position, and landlords generally expect at least some tenants to respond with questions.

The reason negotiation can work is simple economics. When you leave, the landlord faces an empty unit, advertising costs, showings, screening, cleaning and possibly weeks without rent. A good tenant who pays on time and takes care of the place is worth keeping, and a modest concession is often cheaper than turnover. The exact numbers depend on your local market, which is why a calm, prepared approach beats an emotional one.

Negotiation is not only about the monthly rent. You can also ask about the length of the term, the renewal fee, pet and parking charges, a repair you have been waiting on, the notice period, and whether the auto-renewal clause works in your favor or against you. Sometimes a landlord who will not move on rent will agree to a longer term at a locked price, or to a repair or upgrade.

This playbook is built around three ideas. First, start earlier than the offer deadline so you have leverage and options. Second, bring evidence rather than complaints. Third, always offer the landlord an easy yes, such as a signed longer term, in exchange for the concession you want. It is general information, not legal advice, and local rules on rent increases and notice vary, so check what applies where you live.

## Step 1: Read your lease and find the real deadlines

Before you write anything, pull out the lease and locate three items: the end date, the notice deadline for non-renewal and the renewal or holdover terms. If the lease renews automatically, note how many days before the end you must give written notice to leave, and how the notice must be delivered. Our guide to [auto-renewal clauses in leases](/blog/auto-renewal-clauses-in-leases) explains why that date matters. Also check whether the landlord must give you notice of a rent increase and how much, since many places set minimum notice periods for increases. The [notice period calculator](/tools/notice-period-calculator) can turn those dates into a calendar.

Write down two dates on a single page: the date by which you must decide, and the date by which the landlord must tell you the new rent. Everything else is planned backwards from them.

## Step 2: Build your evidence file

Spend an hour gathering facts. A short file is far more persuasive than a long complaint.

1. **Comparable listings.** Find at least three similar units nearby, same bedroom count and roughly the same condition. Save links or screenshots with the date, rent, and any incentives such as a free month.
2. **Your tenant record.** On-time payments, no complaints, no lease violations, any repairs you handled yourself, and how long you have lived there.
3. **Landlord's cost of turnover.** You cannot know the exact figure, but you can list the obvious items: days vacant, cleaning, painting, listing and showing time.
4. **Open issues.** Unfixed repairs or reduced services, with dates you reported them. Keep these as separate requests so they do not sound like threats.
5. **Your walk-away number.** Decide the maximum rent you would accept, and what you would do if you could not reach it. Moving costs money and time, so be honest with yourself.

## Step 3: Pick your ask

Decide what you are asking for before you write. Good asks are specific and easy to say yes to. Examples include holding the rent flat, capping the increase at a smaller amount, waiving a renewal fee, locking the rent for a longer term, adding a repair or repaint, or switching to a lease that renews month to month if you need flexibility. Rank them. Lead with your first choice and keep a fallback ready.

## Step 4: Timeline

A workable schedule looks like this, adjusted to your lease.

- **90 to 120 days before the end:** Review the lease, build the evidence file and send a short note saying you intend to stay and would like to discuss renewal. Many landlords have not even started planning yet, and that is to your advantage.
- **60 to 75 days before the end:** If you have not received an offer, ask for it. If you have, reply within a few days with your counter.
- **45 to 60 days before the end:** Negotiate by email so there is a written record, with at most one call.
- **30 days before the end or your notice deadline, whichever is earlier:** Decide. Sign if you reached a fair deal. If not, send proper written notice and begin looking elsewhere.

Never let the notice deadline pass while you are still talking. A verbal promise does not replace written notice if your lease requires it.

## Step 5: Email scripts

Adapt these to your situation. Keep the tone friendly and factual. Replace the bracketed items, and avoid exaggeration.

### Script A: The opening note (90 to 120 days out)

> Subject: Lease renewal for [unit number]
>
> Hi [Landlord name],
>
> I have enjoyed living at [address] and would like to stay when my lease ends on [date]. Could you let me know what renewal terms you are planning and when I can expect the offer? I would also like to talk about the length of the term. Thank you, and let me know if a quick call is easier.
>
> Best,
> [Your name]

### Script B: The counteroffer (after you receive their number)

> Subject: Re: Renewal offer for [unit number]
>
> Hi [Landlord name],
>
> Thank you for sending the renewal offer of [new rent]. I would like to renew, and I am hoping we can find a number that works for both of us.
>
> I have paid on time every month for [number] months and have kept the unit in good condition. I also looked at similar units nearby, and several are listed between [low] and [high], with some offering [incentive]. Because of that, I would like to propose renewing at [your number] for a [12 or 18]-month term.
>
> A longer term with a reliable tenant also saves you vacancy and turnover costs. Would that work? I am happy to sign promptly once we agree.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### Script C: The fallback when they say no

> Subject: Re: Renewal offer for [unit number]
>
> Hi [Landlord name],
>
> I understand the rent needs to move. I would still like to stay, so let me suggest a middle option. Could we do [middle number], or keep the rent at [number] and have [repair or upgrade] completed before the new term begins? If a longer term helps, I can commit to [length].
>
> If that does not work, please let me know by [date] so I can plan accordingly.
>
> Thanks again,
> [Your name]

## Step 6: Handle common responses

**"The market has gone up."** Ask which comparables they used, and share yours politely. Avoid arguing about the market in general. Keep to specific units.

**"This is our standard increase."** Ask whether there is flexibility on term length, fees or repairs. Standard on rent does not always mean standard on everything.

**"Take it or leave it."** Stay calm. Ask for the final offer in writing and a deadline, then compare it with your walk-away number and your real moving costs.

**Silence.** Follow up once after three to five business days, then check your lease for what happens at the end of the term. Some leases roll into expensive month-to-month terms.

## Step 7: Get it in writing

Once you agree, ask for a signed renewal or lease amendment that states the rent, term, start date, and any extra promises such as repairs, waived fees or locked parking. Check that the renewal does not add new clauses by running it through the [clause scanner](/tools/clause-scanner) or [rental lease scanner](/tools/rental-lease-scanner). Look for fresh fees, a longer notice requirement, or a changed auto-renewal clause. Keep a copy.

## What to avoid

- Threatening to leave when you do not intend to.
- Missing your own notice deadline.
- Making the conversation personal or listing every grievance at once.
- Agreeing to a fast verbal deal and skipping the written paperwork.
- Assuming rules about increases or retaliation are the same everywhere. Look up your local rules, and speak to a tenant organization if you believe the landlord is acting unfairly.

A respectful, prepared and well-timed request will not work every time, but it costs little, and even a small concession on rent or terms can add up over a year.
