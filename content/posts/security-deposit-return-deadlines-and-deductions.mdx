When you move out, the security deposit is often the biggest single sum of money still tied up in your old home. Understanding how return deadlines and deductions typically work helps you spot a bad deduction and respond with confidence. This article explains the common patterns. It is general information, not legal advice, and the details change depending on where you live, so confirm the rules for your state, province or city.

## How the return timeline usually works

Many US states set a maximum number of days for a landlord to return your deposit or send a written explanation of deductions after you move out. The length of that window varies from place to place, and some jurisdictions add stricter rules for landlords who miss it, such as losing the right to deduct anything or owing extra money to the tenant. Your lease may state a period, but it generally cannot be longer than what local rules allow.

Three practical points matter more than the exact number of days.

1. **When the clock starts.** It typically starts when the tenancy ends and you hand back possession, not when the lease term technically expires. Returning keys and getting that acknowledged in writing gives you a clear start date.
2. **Your forwarding address.** Some places require the tenant to provide one before the landlord must send anything. Email it, and keep a copy.
3. **What counts as a response.** Many rules ask for either the money or an itemized written statement. A vague note saying "we kept it for repairs" often falls short.

## Normal wear and tear versus damage

This is the most argued-over line in deposit disputes. Normal wear and tear is the gradual deterioration that happens when people live in a place, even careful people. Damage is harm beyond that, caused by accident, neglect or misuse.

Examples that are commonly treated as normal wear and tear:

- Faded paint or small scuffs where furniture sat
- Minor carpet matting in walkways
- A few small nail or tack holes from hanging pictures
- Worn finish on hardwood floors
- Loose door handles or sticky drawers from age

Examples that are commonly treated as damage:

- Large holes in walls, or wall paint in colors you chose without permission
- Burns, pet stains or deep tears in carpet
- Broken windows, doors or fixtures
- Missing appliances or parts
- Excess dirt, grease or trash left behind

Length of tenancy matters. A carpet that is ten years old has little remaining value, so a landlord who charges a tenant the full replacement price after a one-year stay is asking for more than the tenant caused. Many dispute-resolution bodies look at the age and expected lifespan of the item. Ask for that reasoning if a deduction looks high.

## Typical deductions you may see

An itemized statement commonly includes some of these categories.

### Unpaid rent and fees

Rent owed under the lease and agreed late fees can usually be taken from the deposit. Ask for a ledger showing the months and amounts.

### Cleaning

Cleaning is usually allowed when the unit is left dirtier than when you moved in, not because the landlord prefers to clean between tenants. Photos from move-out are your best counter-evidence.

### Repairs

Repair charges should relate to actual damage, with a receipt, invoice or at least a written estimate. Be cautious about vague entries such as "maintenance, $400."

### Rekeying, trash removal and other items

Charges for lost keys, rekeying after a lost key, or removing abandoned items can be fair if documented. Routine turnover costs, like rekeying between every tenant, are more often treated as the landlord's own expense.

## What a risky lease clause looks like

Some leases try to shift the balance before any dispute begins. Here is a fictional example of a clause that gives the landlord a lot of room:

> Example clause: "Landlord shall have up to ninety (90) days after Tenant vacates to return the Security Deposit. Tenant is responsible for all cleaning, painting and carpet replacement costs regardless of condition, and Landlord's determination of such costs shall be final."

Two things stand out. The return window is far longer than many places allow, and "Landlord's determination shall be final" removes your right to challenge the amounts. ## Step-by-step: what to do at move-out

1. **Read your lease and local rules** for the return deadline and any notice you must give before moving out.
2. **Clean the unit** to the standard you received it in, and take wide and close-up photos or a walk-through video with timestamps.
3. **Request a pre-move-out inspection** if your area allows one. Some landlords will list problems you can fix before you leave.
4. **Return the keys** and get written acknowledgment, even a reply email.
5. **Send your forwarding address** in writing.
6. **Calendar the deadline** and wait for the statement.
7. **Compare every line** with your photos, move-in checklist and the lease.

## If the deductions look wrong

Start with a short, polite written dispute. Point to specific line items, attach your photos and ask for supporting receipts. Many disagreements end here because landlords know documentation is thin.

If that fails, a demand letter that cites the missed deadline or the unsupported deductions can help, and the next step is often small claims court. Filing costs and procedures vary, and many courts are set up so that tenants can handle these cases without a lawyer. Some places allow tenants to recover more than the deposit when a landlord wrongfully withholds it, but that depends entirely on local rules, so look up yours before you assume.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a landlord have to return a security deposit?

It depends on where you live. Many US states set a fixed number of days after move-out, and the length differs from place to place. A lease usually cannot extend the period beyond what local rules allow, so look up your local deadline and write down the day you returned the keys.

### Can a landlord deduct for normal wear and tear?

Generally no, although the exact rule depends on your location. Normal wear and tear covers gradual deterioration like faded paint or light carpet matting. Deductions are usually reserved for damage, unpaid rent and cleaning beyond ordinary tidiness, and landlords are often expected to document them.

### What if my landlord does not send an itemized list?

Many places treat a missing or late itemized statement as a serious problem for the landlord, sometimes limiting their right to keep any of the deposit. Send a written reminder with your forwarding address and the deadline you believe applies, then consider small claims court if there is no response.

### Does my lease deadline override local law?

Usually not. Local rules about deposits are often mandatory, meaning a lease clause that gives the landlord more time or fewer duties may not be enforceable. Treat your lease as a starting point and verify the legal minimums where you live.
