Life changes faster than a twelve-month lease. A new job, a family emergency or a bad living situation can leave you needing to move out months before the term ends. What you owe at that point depends on the exact wording of your lease and on local rules, and the difference between the options can be thousands of dollars. This article explains the three structures that most often appear, what each costs, and what to ask for. It is general information, not legal advice.

## The three common structures

Most early-exit language falls into one of these buckets.

### 1. A fixed break fee

Some leases offer a clean way out for a stated price, often described as a set number of months of rent plus a notice period.

> Example clause: "Tenant may terminate this Lease before the end of the term by giving at least sixty (60) days' written notice and paying a termination fee equal to two (2) months' rent. Upon payment, Tenant shall have no further obligation for rent."

This is the friendliest structure because the cost is known. Check three things: whether the fee is due with the notice or at move-out, whether the notice period counts from delivery or from the next rent date, and whether you still owe rent during the notice period on top of the fee. The [notice period calculator](/tools/notice-period-calculator) can help you map the dates.

### 2. Liability for the remaining rent

Many leases say nothing about a fee and simply make you responsible for rent through the end of the term.

> Example clause: "If Tenant vacates before the expiration of the term, Tenant shall remain liable for all rent due through the end of the term, plus any costs incurred by Landlord in reletting the Premises."

On paper, this can sound devastating, but in many places a landlord is expected to make reasonable efforts to find a new tenant rather than let the unit sit empty and bill you for all of it. This idea is often called mitigation of damages. Whether it applies, and how strict it is, depends on where you live, so check your local tenant rules rather than assuming either way.

### 3. Reletting fees and costs

Some leases combine a modest charge with an obligation to cover the landlord's expenses in finding a replacement.

> Example clause: "Tenant shall pay a reletting fee equal to one (1) month's rent, plus advertising costs and any rent loss until a new tenant takes possession, whichever is greater."

Ask what "costs" means. A flat fee plus uncapped rent loss until the unit is re-rented is a very different risk than a flat fee that ends your liability. Also ask whether the reletting fee is reduced if you find a qualified replacement yourself.

## Clauses worth reading twice

These phrases hide the most cost.

- **"Whichever is greater."** It guarantees the landlord the higher number.
- **"Landlord has no duty to relet."** Some leases try to waive the landlord's effort to find a new tenant. Whether that is enforceable varies by location.
- **"Acceleration."** The landlord may demand all remaining rent at once. Ask whether the amount is reduced when the unit is re-rented.
- **"Liquidated damages."** A pre-set penalty. In some places a penalty that is far out of proportion to the actual loss can be challenged, but do not assume that without checking.
- **Application to the deposit.** Make sure the fee is not also deducted from the deposit on top of what you pay directly.

## Alternatives to paying the full penalty

Before you accept a bill, consider these routes.

1. **Sublet or assign.** Some leases let you sublet with the landlord's consent. Check whether consent can be unreasonably withheld. If you find a solid replacement, your remaining liability may drop or disappear.
2. **Lease takeover or transfer.** A new tenant signs a new lease and yours ends. Ask whether the landlord charges a transfer fee and whether it is a fixed amount.
3. **Negotiate a buyout.** Landlords often prefer a certain, quick resolution over weeks of vacancy and advertising. A written offer with a firm move-out date and a clean, empty unit can be persuasive.
4. **Statutory early-exit rights.** Some places allow early termination without penalty in specific situations, such as military deployment, domestic violence, or serious habitability problems the landlord has not fixed. These rules differ widely and usually have strict notice and paperwork requirements, so look up your local rules before relying on them.

## How to approach the conversation

Put everything in writing and keep it short. Say clearly that you need to leave, give the date, and ask what the landlord requires. Offer practical help, such as allowing showings, leaving the unit clean and sharing photos for the listing. Do not simply stop paying rent and disappear. That can lead to a collections account, a judgment, or a rental history entry that follows you to your next application.

Ask for any agreement in a signed document that says the lease ends on a specific date and that you owe nothing further beyond the amount stated. A verbal promise is hard to prove later. Review the actual wording of your lease. Look for a termination clause, a subletting clause, a default clause and a deposit clause, because they work together. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to pay the rest of my lease if I move out early?

Not always, but sometimes. Many leases make you liable for rent through the end of the term, but in many places the landlord must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit, which can reduce what you owe. The rules depend on where you live and on your lease wording, so check both.

### What is a reletting fee?

It is a charge the landlord collects to cover the effort and cost of finding a replacement tenant, such as advertising, showings and screening. Some leases set it at a flat amount, often described as a portion of a month's rent, while others make you pay actual costs. Ask for the amount in writing.

### Can I find my own replacement tenant?

Often yes, if the lease allows subletting or assignment or if the landlord agrees. Landlords usually have the right to screen a replacement the same way they screen anyone else. Provide a complete application and make the landlord's job easy, which increases the chance they approve the swap.

### Will leaving early hurt my credit or rental history?

It can if unpaid amounts are sent to collections or reported, or if the landlord obtains a judgment. Leaving with a signed agreement and no balance generally avoids that. Always get a written release or a zero-balance letter so you can show a future landlord that the matter was settled.
